# 5.1 — Explain the troubleshooting methodology

**Domain 5 is 24% of the exam — the largest single domain — and this objective is its spine.** Every other Domain 5 topic is a symptom or a tool; this is the order in which they get used.

**⚠️ The exam tests the order, not just the steps.** A question describing an engineer who replaced a switch before testing a theory is asking which step was skipped. **Learn the sequence as a sequence.**

## The seven steps

```
1  Identify the problem
2  Establish a theory of probable cause
3  Test the theory to determine the cause
4  Establish a plan of action, and identify potential effects
5  Implement the solution, or escalate
6  Verify full system functionality, and apply preventive measures
7  Document findings, actions, outcomes and lessons learned
```

**Step 7 is not last in practice.** The official wording is *"document ... throughout the process"* — notes taken during the work, not written from memory afterwards.

**The loop worth noticing: step 3 can send you back to step 2.** A theory that fails is not a wasted step; it eliminates a cause. **The methodology is iterative, and the exam expects you to return to theory rather than jump to a fix.**

---

## Step 1 — Identify the problem

**The step most often skipped, and the one that makes every later step cheaper.**

### Gather information

What is affected, what is not, and when it started. **The boundary between working and broken is usually where the fault lives.**

### Question users

**The report is a symptom, not a diagnosis.** "The internet is down" routinely means one site, one application, or name resolution — three different faults with the same sentence attached.

**Ask what they were doing, what they expected, and what they saw instead.** Users describe consequences; you need observations.

### Identify symptoms

**Separate what is observed from what is inferred.** "The server is down" is an inference. "The page times out from my desk" is an observation. **Inferences inherited from a reporter are the commonest way an investigation starts in the wrong place.**

### Determine if anything has changed

**The highest-yield question in the whole methodology.** Most faults follow a change — a configuration edit, an update, a new device, an expired certificate.

**⚠️ And the commonest trap.** Correlation with a change window is a strong prior and not a conclusion: a change made an hour before a symptom appears is worth suspecting and still has to be tested.

**"Nothing changed" is frequently wrong rather than dishonest** — automatic updates, certificate expiry and scheduled tasks are changes nobody made.

### Duplicate the problem, if possible

**A fault you can reproduce is a fault you can test.** It also tells you the conditions that trigger it, which narrows the cause on its own.

**⚠️ If it cannot be reproduced, that is information too** — intermittent, load-dependent, or specific to one user, device or path.

### Approach multiple problems individually

**Two faults at once produce symptoms that fit neither.** Separate them, or every theory tested will half-work and mislead.

---

## Step 2 — Establish a theory of probable cause

### Question the obvious

**Check the cheap and likely before the clever and unlikely.** A disconnected cable, a powered-off device, a disabled interface, a mistyped password, the wrong port.

**The reason this is a named step is that experience works against it.** Someone who knows routing protocols reaches for a routing explanation, and spends an hour on a theory a thirty-second check would have eliminated.

### Consider multiple approaches

**The exam names three, and expects you to pick one from the symptoms:**

| Approach | Start at | Best when |
|---|---|---|
| **Bottom-to-top** | **Layer 1** — cabling, link lights, interface state | Physical symptoms, or **many users affected at once** |
| **Top-to-bottom** | **Layer 7** — the application | **One application fails while everything else works** |
| **Divide and conquer** | **The middle** — Layer 3 or 4. Can it ping? Can it resolve? | **No strong prior.** The result sends you up or down |

**Divide and conquer is the efficient default**, because a single test at Layer 3 eliminates half the stack either way. **Bottom-to-top is the safe default** when symptoms are vague, because Layer 1 faults are common and cheap to check.

**The OSI model is being used as a search strategy here**, not as theory — which is the most practical use it has.

---

## Step 3 — Test the theory to determine the cause

**Testing is not fixing.** A good test **distinguishes between candidate causes**, cheaply and reversibly. Replacing a switch is not a test; unplugging one cable is.

**Two outcomes, and the exam names both:**

| Outcome | Next |
|---|---|
| **Theory confirmed** | Determine the next steps to resolve the problem — **step 4, not an immediate fix** |
| **Theory not confirmed** | **Establish a new theory, or escalate** |

**⚠️ Escalation is a listed outcome, not a failure.** Escalate when the cause sits outside your access, authority or expertise, or when the time to continue exceeds the value of solving it yourself. **An exam scenario describing someone struggling past their remit is usually testing whether you recognise that.**

### Change one thing at a time

**The discipline that makes a test mean anything.** Two changes and a working system tells you nothing about which one worked — and one of them may have introduced a fault that appears next week.

**A controlled test is worth more than a config review.** Turning a suspected cause off, observing, turning it back on and observing again produces evidence. **Reading a configuration produces an opinion**, and a configuration that reads correctly and behaves incorrectly is common enough to be the normal case rather than the exception.

**A failed theory is not a wasted step — it eliminates a cause.** The loop back to step 2 is part of the method, not a sign of doing it badly.

---

## Step 4 — Establish a plan of action, and identify potential effects

**Two halves, and the second is what makes it a plan rather than an intention.**

**The plan:** what will be done, in what order, and how it will be undone.

**The potential effects:** what the fix itself might break. **Who loses service while it happens, what depends on the thing being changed, and how long the window needs to be.**

**⚠️ A fix with a larger blast radius than the fault is a bad fix.** Rebooting a core switch at midday to clear one user's problem is technically a solution and practically an outage.

**This is where change management meets troubleshooting** — approval, a window, and a rollback path, exactly as objective 3.1 describes for planned changes. **An urgent fault does not remove the need for them; it compresses the time available to arrange them.**

## Step 5 — Implement the solution, or escalate as necessary

**Escalation appears a second time, deliberately.** A cause you correctly identified can still need someone else to fix it — vendor support, physical access, another team's system, a change window you cannot authorise.

**Implementing includes staying within the plan.** Discovering a second problem mid-fix is a reason to return to step 1 for that problem, not to start improvising.

## Step 6 — Verify full system functionality, and apply preventive measures

**"Full" is the operative word.** Not just the reported symptom — **what you changed, and what depends on it.**

**⚠️ Verifying only the original complaint is the classic incomplete fix.** A firewall rule that restores one service and silently blocks another passes that check and fails the next one.

**Have the user confirm.** They reported the symptom; they are the ones who can say it is gone.

**Preventive measures, where they apply:** monitoring that would have caught it sooner, a configuration change that removes the cause, or documentation so the next person does not repeat the diagnosis.

**This is the step that turns a fix into an improvement.** Without it, the same fault returns and costs the same hour.

## Step 7 — Document findings, actions, outcomes and lessons learned

**Throughout the process**, per the official wording — notes taken while working, not reconstructed afterwards. **Memory is worst at exactly the details that matter:** what was checked and ruled out, and in what order.

**What earns its place:**

| Record | Because |
|---|---|
| **The symptom as reported** | It is rarely what the fault turned out to be, and that gap is useful next time |
| **What was checked and eliminated** | **Stops the next person repeating the dead ends** |
| **The actual cause** | |
| **What was changed** | The first thing to look at when something breaks next week |
| **How it was verified** | Distinguishes a fix from a coincidence |
| **Lessons learned** | The only part that improves anything |

**⚠️ Documentation is also the step with no immediate reward**, which is why it is the one most often skipped — and why the objective lists it explicitly rather than assuming it.

**The value is asymmetric and delayed.** It costs ten minutes now and saves an hour on a repeat, for someone who may be you.
