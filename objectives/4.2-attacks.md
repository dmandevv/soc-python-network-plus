# 4.2 — Summarize various types of attacks and their impact to the network

## Denial-of-service (DoS) / distributed denial-of-service (DDoS)

**Target: availability.** Every attack in this group is an attack on the A in CIA.

| | **DoS** | **DDoS** |
|---|---|---|
| Sources | One | **Many** — usually a botnet |
| Blocking the source | Works | **Doesn't** — thousands of addresses, many of them innocent compromised devices |

### Three categories, by what gets exhausted

| Category | Exhausts | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Volumetric** | **Bandwidth** — the pipe itself | UDP floods, amplification attacks |
| **Protocol** | **State tables** on firewalls, load balancers, servers | **SYN flood** — half-open connections fill the table |
| **Application layer** | **Server resources** behind a working connection | HTTP floods, Slowloris |

**⚠️ The volumetric trap: you cannot firewall your way out at your own edge.** By the time the traffic reaches your firewall, your uplink is already saturated — the firewall drops packets perfectly on a link that has no room left for legitimate ones. **Mitigation has to happen upstream**: the ISP, a scrubbing service, or a CDN absorbing it before it reaches you.

### Amplification (reflection)

The attacker sends small requests to open servers with the **victim's address spoofed as the source**. The servers send large replies to the victim.

| Reflector | Why it's abused |
|---|---|
| **DNS** (open resolvers) | Small query, large response — especially `ANY` |
| **NTP** (`monlist`) | Returned the last 600 clients — amplification in the hundreds |
| **Memcached** | Amplification factors above 10,000× |
| **SSDP** | Consumer devices answering on the internet by mistake |

**⚠️ Reflection needs UDP.** TCP's handshake means a spoofed source never completes the connection, so the server never sends the payload. UDP answers whoever the packet *claims* to be from — which is the whole mechanism.

**The fix at the source is BCP 38 ingress filtering**: networks dropping outbound packets whose source address could not have originated inside them. Widely recommended, unevenly deployed — which is why amplification still works.

**Usually implemented as uRPF** (unicast Reverse Path Forwarding): drop any packet whose source would not route back out the interface it arrived on. **Same principle as binding a firewall rule's source address to an interface, in the other direction** — BCP 38 stops you *emitting* spoofed traffic and protects everyone else; an interface-bound rule stops you *trusting* it and protects you.

*Homelab: `in-interface=vlan10` on the management rule is the per-rule version. `/ip settings set rp-filter=strict` is the blanket version — and it would stop a compromised sandbox VM sending forged sources out through the lab, which is BCP 38 at home.*

### Mitigations worth matching to type

| Type | Control |
|---|---|
| Volumetric | **Upstream** scrubbing, CDN, anycast |
| SYN flood | **SYN cookies** — no state stored until the handshake completes |
| Application layer | Rate limiting, WAF, challenge pages |

*Homelab: the website sits behind a Cloudflare Tunnel, so volumetric traffic lands on Cloudflare's network rather than your uplink. That's the "mitigate upstream" answer, in place by accident of architecture.*

*And yesterday's STP lab showed the Layer 2 version: a switching loop is a self-inflicted DoS — broadcasts circulating until the fabric is saturated.*

## VLAN hopping

**Target: segmentation.** Reaching a VLAN you were never assigned to. Two methods, and the discriminator is direction.

| | **Switch spoofing** | **Double tagging** |
|---|---|---|
| How | Attacker's port negotiates itself into a **trunk** via DTP | Frame carries **two 802.1Q tags** — outer is the native VLAN, inner is the target |
| Requires | A port left in a dynamic trunking mode | Attacker on the **native VLAN**, target reached **across a trunk** |
| Traffic | **Bidirectional** — the attacker is now on every VLAN | **One-way only** — replies can't come back |
| Fix | `switchport mode access` + `switchport nonegotiate` | Native VLAN set to an **unused VLAN with no hosts**, or tag the native VLAN |

**How double tagging works:** the first switch sees the outer tag matches the native VLAN, strips it (native traffic crosses trunks untagged), and forwards the frame. The second switch reads the now-exposed inner tag and delivers the frame into the target VLAN.

**⚠️ One-way is the exam point.** Double tagging can't carry a conversation, because the target's reply has no way back. It's useful for blind attacks and DoS, not for anything interactive. If a question describes an attacker getting *responses* from another VLAN, it's switch spoofing.

**⚠️ DTP (Dynamic Trunking Protocol) is Cisco-proprietary.** Switch spoofing depends on it, so it's a Cisco-environment attack.

### The two fixes, and why you want both

| Fix | Does | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **Native VLAN = an unused VLAN with no hosts** | Removes the launch point. The untagged hop still exists, but belongs to a VLAN nobody can reach | **Fragile and silent.** Assign a port to that VLAN later and the attack is live again, with nothing in the config looking wrong |
| **`vlan dot1q tag native`** (global) | Tags native traffic too, so **nothing crosses a trunk untagged** — removes the mechanism rather than the target | Not universally supported; must match on both ends of every trunk |

**Verified in `vlan-hopping-lab.pkt`, 2026-09-10.** Two switches, a trunk, and one conversation in each of two VLANs. Inspecting frames on the trunk in Simulation mode:

- **Native VLAN frame: nothing between SRC ADDR and TYPE.** The 802.1Q tag is four bytes inserted exactly there — TPID `0x8100` plus TCI holding the VLAN ID — so an empty gap means the frame carries no VLAN identity at all, and the receiving switch can only assume.
- **Non-native frame: `0x8100` present.**
- **After moving native to an empty VLAN 999, the VLAN 10 frame became tagged** — the fix made visible, with nothing about VLAN 10 itself changed.

**⚠️ Two Packet Tracer limits found:**
- **The attack cannot be executed** — no way to emit a frame with two 802.1Q tags. Neither a PC nor Add Complex PDU has a second tag field
- **`vlan dot1q tag native` does not exist** in Packet Tracer, so the stronger fix is not testable there

*Homelab: both are already closed on the CRS326. RouterOS has no DTP, so there's nothing to spoof. And native VLAN 99 has no address and no devices — that's the double-tagging mitigation from the table, in place since Phase 1.*

## MAC flooding

*MAC here is Media Access Control — the other MAC from 4.1.*

The attacker floods the switch with frames from thousands of random source MAC addresses (the classic tool is `macof`). The **CAM table** (Content Addressable Memory — the switch's MAC address table) fills up, and the switch can no longer learn where hosts are.

**It then fails open**: unknown-unicast frames are flooded out every port in the VLAN, so the switch **behaves like a hub** — and the attacker receives traffic meant for other hosts.

**⚠️ It's a confidentiality attack disguised as a DoS.** The flooding looks like a resource-exhaustion attack, but the *impact* is eavesdropping. The switch chose availability (keep forwarding) over confidentiality (forward only where you should), and that choice is the vulnerability.

**⚠️ It stays inside the VLAN.** Flooding happens per broadcast domain, so segmentation limits the blast radius — the attacker sees their own VLAN's traffic, not the whole switch's.

| Mitigation | How |
|---|---|
| **Port security** | Cap the number of MAC addresses learned per port; violation action shuts the port, restricts, or drops |
| **802.1X** | The port doesn't forward at all until the device authenticates |

Both are objective 4.3 — MAC flooding is the attack that justifies port security existing.

## ARP poisoning and ARP spoofing

CompTIA lists these as two terms. **In practice everyone uses them interchangeably**, but the distinction they intend is the same shape as vulnerability versus exploit:

| Term | Is |
|---|---|
| **ARP spoofing** | The **act** — sending forged ARP messages claiming an IP that isn't yours |
| **ARP poisoning** | The **result** — the victim's ARP cache now holds a false mapping |

Spoofing is what the attacker does; poisoning is what the victim ends up with.

### Why it works

**ARP has no authentication whatsoever.** Any host may send an ARP reply at any time, and every host on the segment believes it. **Gratuitous ARP** — an unsolicited reply — is a legitimate feature used for failover and duplicate address detection, and it is also the delivery mechanism for the attack.

The attacker sends two lies:

- To the **victim**: "the gateway's IP is at my MAC"
- To the **gateway**: "the victim's IP is at my MAC"

Both update their caches, and traffic in **both directions** now flows through the attacker. That is an on-path position obtained without compromising either endpoint — only by being on the same segment.

### ⚠️ It cannot cross a router

**ARP is Layer 2 and stops at the broadcast domain.** An attacker can only poison hosts in their own VLAN. This is the single most important constraint, and it is why segmentation is a real mitigation rather than a hopeful one.

### Impact spans all three of CIA

| Leg | How |
|---|---|
| **Confidentiality** | Read the traffic |
| **Integrity** | Modify it in flight |
| **Availability** | Point the IP at a MAC that doesn't exist — traffic goes nowhere |

### ⚠️ HTTPS does not prevent it

Encryption limits what the attacker can *read*; it does not remove them from the path. They still see **which** hosts are talked to, DNS queries, TLS SNI, packet sizes and timing — and they can attempt downgrades or serve their own certificate hoping for a click-through.

**The position is the compromise. Encryption reduces the payoff.**

### Mitigations

| Control | How |
|---|---|
| **Dynamic ARP Inspection (DAI)** | Validates ARP packets against the **DHCP snooping** binding table and drops mismatches. **Requires DHCP snooping to be running first** |
| **Static ARP entries** | Works, doesn't scale |
| **802.1X** | The attacker never gets onto the segment |
| **Segmentation** | Contains it to one VLAN |

DAI and DHCP snooping are objective 4.3. **Detection** is simpler: one MAC address suddenly answering for several IPs, or the gateway's MAC changing — which is what tools like `arpwatch` alert on.

*Homelab: VLAN 50 holds one host, so there is nobody in it to poison — only the gateway. A segment with a single occupant has almost no ARP attack surface, which is a side effect of segmentation rather than its purpose.*

## DNS poisoning and DNS spoofing

Another pair CompTIA splits, and here the split carries real weight:

| Term | Scope | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **DNS spoofing** | Forging **a reply to one query** — answer before the real resolver does | **Transient.** One victim, one lookup |
| **DNS cache poisoning** | Getting a false record **stored in a resolver's cache** | **Persistent and shared.** Everyone using that resolver, for the record's TTL |

**Poisoning is usually achieved by spoofing** — you forge a reply *in order to* get it cached. The difference is whether the lie is consumed once or stored and served repeatedly.

### ⚠️ The TTL is the attacker's dwell time

A poisoned entry survives for whatever **TTL** (Time To Live) the forged record carries — and the attacker chooses it. Days is possible. **The attack is over in milliseconds; the effect is not.**

### How off-path poisoning works — the Kaminsky attack

An attacker who cannot see the query has to **guess** the transaction ID and source port and beat the real answer back to the resolver. That sounds hopeless, and Dan Kaminsky's 2008 insight was that it isn't:

- **Query for random nonexistent subdomains** (`a1b2c3.example.com`) — each one is a fresh race, so the attacker gets **unlimited attempts** instead of one
- **Put the payload in the authority/additional section** — a forged NS record poisons **the whole domain**, not the single name you raced for

**The mitigation was source port randomisation** on top of transaction ID randomisation, taking the guess space from ~16 bits to ~32.

**⚠️ This is why a constant DNS transaction ID is an indicator.** A real resolver randomises it precisely because of this attack — so hundreds of queries sharing one transaction ID means whatever generated them is not a resolver.

### On-path spoofing is far easier

If the attacker can **see** the query, no guessing is needed — just answer first. **ARP poisoning is the usual way to get there**, which is why these two attacks appear together.

### Mitigations, and the trap in them

| Control | Provides | Does **not** provide |
|---|---|---|
| **DNSSEC** | **Origin authentication and integrity** — records are signed, the resolver validates | **Confidentiality.** DNSSEC does not encrypt anything |
| **DoT / DoH** | **Confidentiality and integrity of the channel** | **Authenticity of the record.** A lying server lies over TLS just fine |

**⚠️ They solve different problems and neither replaces the other.** DNSSEC proves *the record is genuine*; DoT and DoH prove *nobody read or altered it in transit*. A question describing eavesdropping on lookups wants DoT/DoH; a question describing a forged record wants DNSSEC.

Also: randomised source ports and transaction IDs, not running an open resolver, and restricting recursion to your own clients.

*Homelab: the switch resolves for VLANs 10–40, so poisoning its cache would affect every segment at once. The Phase 3 logged resolver is what would let you notice.*
