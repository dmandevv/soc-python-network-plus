# 2.4 — Explain important factors of physical installations

## Locations

### Main distribution frame (MDF)
The **central termination point for a building's network** — where the service provider's circuit arrives, where the core and distribution equipment lives, and where all backbone cabling originates. A building normally has one.

**It typically contains:** the **demarcation point** (where the provider's responsibility ends and the organisation's begins), the router or firewall, core switching, and the head end of every backbone run to the intermediate closets. Server and telephony equipment often share the space.

### Intermediate distribution frame (IDF)
A **secondary wiring closet serving a floor or section** of a building, connected back to the MDF by backbone cabling. It houses **access switches** and the patch panels terminating the horizontal cable runs to nearby work areas.

**The relationship is a hierarchical star:** the MDF at the centre, IDFs radiating outward, and work-area cabling radiating from each IDF. This maps directly onto the three-tier model — **core and distribution in the MDF, access in the IDFs**.

### ⚠️ Why IDFs exist — the 100 metre limit
Twisted-pair horizontal cabling is limited to **100 metres total**: a 90 m permanent link plus roughly 10 m of patch cords at each end. Beyond that, signal attenuation and timing make the link unreliable.

**That single constraint forces the architecture.** Any building whose work areas fall outside a 100 m radius of one room cannot be served from a single location. The solution is to place an IDF within 100 m of every work area, then connect each IDF to the MDF with **fibre**, which is not bound by the same distance limit.

**So the hierarchy is not an organisational preference — it is a consequence of copper's distance ceiling.**

### Related structured cabling terms (TIA/EIA-568)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Entrance facility** | Where the provider's cabling enters the building |
| **Equipment room** | The MDF space |
| **Telecommunications room** | An IDF space |
| **Backbone cabling** | MDF ↔ IDF runs, also called vertical or riser cabling — usually fibre |
| **Horizontal cabling** | IDF → work area runs — twisted pair, capped at 90 m |
| **Work area** | The wall outlet and patch cord serving the end device |

**A demarc extension** is cabling that carries the circuit from the demarcation point to the equipment when the provider terminates it somewhere inconvenient — its cost and ownership are a common point of dispute.

**Documentation depends on this naming.** Cable maps identify runs by closet and port (`IDF-2 / patch panel A / port 14`), so a consistent MDF and IDF scheme is what makes physical tracing possible later.

## Rack size

Equipment racks follow the **EIA-310** standard, which fixes two dimensions.

**Width — 19 inches.** This is the width of the equipment's mounting face, not the rack itself; a 19-inch rack is roughly 24 inches wide overall once the frame is included. A **23-inch** variant exists in telecommunications environments.

**Height — measured in rack units.** One **U (or RU)** is **1.75 inches (44.45 mm)**.

| Height | Typical use |
|---|---|
| **42U** | Full-height rack, roughly 6 feet — the data centre standard |
| 45U–48U | Taller data centre cabinets |
| 24U | Half-height, for smaller rooms |
| 6U–12U | **Wall-mounted**, for an IDF or small closet |

Equipment is specified the same way: a **1U** switch, a **2U** server, a **4U** chassis.

**⚠️ Depth is the dimension most often overlooked**, because it is not implied by the other two. Racks are sold in depths from roughly 400 mm to 1200 mm, and equipment varies just as widely — network switches are shallow at 12–18 inches, while servers commonly need 28–32 inches plus clearance for cabling and power at the rear. **A rack that fits a switch perfectly may not accept a server at all.**

**Two-post vs. four-post:**

| | **Two-post (relay rack)** | **Four-post** |
|---|---|---|
| Rails | Two vertical, centre-mounted | Front and rear |
| Suits | Patch panels, switches — light and shallow | **Servers** — heavy and deep |
| Cost and airflow | Cheaper, open on all sides | Heavier frame, often enclosed |

**Open frame vs. enclosed cabinet:** an open frame is cheaper, cools passively, and gives unobstructed access. An enclosed cabinet provides **physical security through locking doors**, keeps dust out, and channels airflow front to back — which is what makes hot-aisle and cold-aisle containment possible.

**Weight capacity** is specified as **static** (stationary) and **dynamic** (while being rolled) load. In older buildings the **floor loading** of a fully populated rack can itself be the limiting factor.

**Plan for spare units.** A rack filled to capacity leaves no room for growth, and no gap for airflow or for the cable management that keeps the installation serviceable.

## Port-side exhaust / intake

The **direction a device moves air through its chassis**, expressed relative to the side the network ports are on.

| Term | Air enters at | Air leaves at |
|---|---|---|
| **Port-side exhaust** | The power supply side | **The port side** |
| **Port-side intake** | **The port side** | The power supply side |

**Why the ports are used as the reference:** "front-to-back" is ambiguous, because which face counts as the front differs between servers and switches and between vendors. The port side is unambiguous on every device, so it became the standard point of reference.

### Hot aisle / cold aisle
Racks are arranged in alternating rows so that equipment **intakes face one another across a cold aisle**, supplied by perforated floor tiles or overhead cooling, and **exhausts face one another across a hot aisle**, where heated air is collected and returned to the cooling plant.

**This only works if every device in a rack moves air the same way.**

**⚠️ A single reversed device draws its neighbour's exhaust as intake air.** It receives air already heated by other equipment rather than the supply temperature, which shortens component life and can trigger thermal shutdown — while the rack's cooling capacity appears adequate on paper.

**Why both variants are sold:** in a typical data centre rack, servers face the cold aisle with their ports at the rear, so a top-of-rack switch must have its ports on the **hot aisle** side to keep cabling short. That switch therefore has to draw air from the cold aisle and exhaust toward its own ports — **port-side exhaust**. Vendors ship the same switch model in both airflow variants, sometimes with reversible fan trays, and **ordering the wrong one cannot be corrected in software**.

**Blanking panels** fill unused rack units so hot air cannot recirculate from the hot aisle back through the rack into the intake side. An open gap short-circuits the containment the whole layout depends on.

**⚠️ Side-to-side airflow**, found on some compact switches, is poorly suited to racks — the intake and exhaust face the rack rails and neighbouring equipment rather than an open aisle.

## Cabling

### Patch panel
A **passive panel of ports that terminates the permanent horizontal cabling** from work areas, presenting each run as a numbered jack on its front face. It contains no electronics, requires no power, and does not appear as a hop in the path.

**The rear** is **punched down** with a punch-down tool onto insulation displacement contacts, following **T568A or T568B**, using solid-core cable. **The front** presents RJ45 jacks, joined to switch ports by flexible stranded **patch cords**.

**⚠️ Why it exists — the reason worth understanding:** the cable inside the wall is **solid-core**, whose conductors fatigue and break when flexed repeatedly, and replacing it means opening walls and ceilings. Terminating it **once** at a panel means every subsequent move, add, or change is performed with a cheap, replaceable patch cord, and **the permanent cable is never disturbed again**.

**Its second purpose is documentation.** Every run appears in one labelled, ordered place, which is what makes tracing a cable months later possible at all.

**Common forms:** 24-port and 48-port, in 1U or 2U; either fixed punch-down panels or **keystone** panels, which accept snap-in modules so RJ45, fibre, and coaxial terminations can share one panel.

**Note that patch cords count against the 100 metre channel limit** — the standard allows roughly 10 m of patch cord across both ends, on top of the 90 m permanent link.

**Common faults:** an incorrect punch-down leaving pairs untwisted for too great a length, **split pairs**, or terminating T568A at one end and T568B at the other, which produces an unintended crossover.

#### Punch-down and IDC
**Punch-down** means terminating a wire by forcing it into a metal slot that cuts through the insulation and grips the copper — **no stripping, no soldering, no screws**. The connector is an **IDC (insulation displacement connector)**: a metal fork whose slot is narrower than the conductor.

**⚠️ The insulation is displaced, not removed.** The fork's edges slice two narrow slits through the plastic **only where the wire crosses the slot**, and contact the copper at those points. The wire remains a continuous, fully insulated wire on both sides of the contact — **no bare conductor is ever exposed**, and nothing further is done to the wire after punching. The only material removed is the offcut trimmed beyond the connector, which is waste.

**That is a deliberate safety property.** Eight conductors sit within a few millimetres of each other on a patch panel; if they were stripped, the assembly would be a shorting hazard. The retained insulation also holds the pair geometry and seals the contact, producing a **gas-tight connection** that resists oxidation for decades — the reason IDC outlasts a screw terminal.

**⚠️ "Gas-tight" describes the microscopic metal-to-metal junction, not the housing.** The blade bites in hard enough that no air gap exists **at the contact point**, so oxygen and moisture cannot reach the freshly cut copper. The connector body itself is not sealed and does not need to be. Note also that **the piercing blade is the conductor** — it is not a clamp holding a separate contact, so the act of piercing and the act of conducting are the same interface, which is why IDC has so few points of failure.

**The tool** has a **spring-loaded impact mechanism** that releases at a set force, so every termination is seated identically regardless of who makes it. Its blade has a **cut** end, which seats the wire and trims the excess in one motion, and a **non-cut** end for daisy-chained blocks; the cut side faces outward. Blade types include **110** (standard in structured cabling), **Krone**, **BIX**, and **66** (older telephone).

**Procedure:** strip roughly one inch of the **outer jacket only**, fan out the four pairs, follow the colour code printed on the panel, lay each wire into its slot, and punch with the cut side outward. **The individual conductors are never stripped** — the IDC does that, and pre-stripping them ruins the termination.

**After punching all eight:** verify the colour code against the panel's printed guide, **secure the cable jacket to the panel with a tie so mechanical strain reaches the tie rather than the contacts**, label the port, and certify the run.

**⚠️ The two faults that matter:**
- **Excessive untwist.** The twist is what cancels crosstalk; untwisting too far at the termination causes **NEXT (near-end crosstalk)** failures. The limit is **0.5 inch (13 mm)** for Cat 5e and Cat 6, and this is the most common quality problem in real installations.
- **Split pairs.** Using two wires from different pairs for one signal pair. All eight conductors connect end to end, so **a basic continuity tester passes it**, but the two conductors are not twisted together and cancel nothing. Only a certification tester detects it.

**Punch down vs. crimp:**

| | **Punch down** | **Crimp** |
|---|---|---|
| Attaches to | **Jack or panel** | **RJ45 plug** |
| Cable | **Solid core** | **Stranded** |
| Tool | Impact tool | Crimper |
| Found on | Wall outlets, patch panels | Patch cords |

**The rule: solid core is punched down, stranded is crimped** — which follows from where each lives, since solid core stays in walls and stranded is flexed daily.

### Fiber distribution panel
The same concept applied to fibre — also called a fibre patch panel, **LIU (light interface unit)**, or fibre enclosure. It terminates the incoming backbone fibre and presents it as **LC or SC adapters** on the front for patch cords.

**Why fibre needs more than a patch panel does:** backbone and outdoor fibre cable is stiff, contains strength members and gel, and cannot be connected to equipment directly. Inside the enclosure, the incoming fibres are **fusion spliced to pigtails** — short factory-terminated lengths whose connectors seat into the panel's adapters. A **splice tray** holds and protects those joints.

**Two physical constraints the enclosure exists to manage:**

- **Minimum bend radius** — fibre bent too tightly suffers macrobend loss and can fracture. The enclosure provides **slack storage** so a service loop can be coiled safely.
- **Contamination** — a dust particle on a ferrule end face is among the most common causes of fibre link failure. Unused adapters are kept capped, and connectors are cleaned and inspected before insertion.

| | **Patch panel** | **Fibre distribution panel** |
|---|---|---|
| Terminates | Twisted pair horizontal runs | **Backbone fibre** |
| Rear termination | **Punch-down** to IDC | **Fusion splice** to pigtails |
| Front interface | RJ45 jacks | **LC / SC adapters** |
| Extra requirements | None | **Bend radius, slack storage, end-face cleanliness** |
| Powered | No | No |

## Lockable

Restricting **physical access** to network infrastructure — the room, the rack, and where necessary the individual device.

**⚠️ The principle behind it: physical access defeats every logical control.** Someone standing at the equipment can connect to the **console port**, which bypasses network authentication entirely; can invoke the device's **password recovery procedure**, which is a documented feature of virtually every switch, router, and firewall and grants full administrative access; can plug into an open port, insert a tap or rogue device, remove storage, or simply cut the power.

**Password recovery is the decisive example.** It is deliberate functionality, not a defect — vendors provide it so that hardware is not lost when credentials are. Its consequence is that **the lock is the authentication.** No configured password protects a device an attacker can hold.

**The layers, outermost first:**

| Layer | Control |
|---|---|
| Building | Staffed entry, badge access |
| Floor / corridor | Badged doors between zones |
| **Room (MDF / IDF)** | **Keyed or badge-controlled door, ideally with a door contact and alarm** |
| **Rack** | **Lockable front and rear doors, and side panels** |
| Device / port | Port blockers, locking cable retainers, cable locks |

**⚠️ Rear access matters at least as much as front access**, because the console port and the power connections are at the back. A cabinet locked only at the front protects the panel that reveals the least.

**Keyed locks provide no audit trail.** Electronic access control records **who entered and when**, which is what regulatory frameworks require and what makes an incident investigable afterwards. Where the log matters, CCTV covering the entry is the usual complement.

**The recurring practical failure is the IDF, not the MDF.** Main equipment rooms are generally treated seriously; intermediate closets are frequently shared with cleaning supplies or building services, propped open during work, or fitted with a lock whose key is widely copied. **An attacker chooses the weakest closet, not the best-defended one** — so the control is only as strong as the least protected location holding live switch ports.

## Power

### Uninterruptible power supply (UPS)
A battery-backed device that supplies power when utility power fails, and conditions it while utility power is present.

**⚠️ It performs two distinct jobs, which are often conflated:**
- **Ride-through** — carrying the load across brief interruptions, so nothing notices
- **Graceful shutdown** — providing enough time to close files and halt cleanly during a longer outage

**A UPS is not a generator.** Runtime is measured in **minutes**, not hours. Sustained operation through a long outage is a generator's role, with the UPS covering the gap until it starts.

**Three topologies:**

| | **Standby (offline)** | **Line-interactive** | **Online (double conversion)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal path | Utility direct to load | Utility through an **autotransformer** | **Always through the inverter** |
| Transfer time | 2–10 ms | Shorter | **Zero** |
| Corrects voltage without battery | No | **Yes (AVR)** | **Yes** |
| Cost and use | Cheapest — desktops | **Most common** — closets, small servers | Most expensive — critical loads |

**Line-interactive adds automatic voltage regulation (AVR)**, correcting brownouts and overvoltage using a transformer tap rather than the battery — which matters because sags are far more frequent than outages, and a standby unit drains its battery for each one.

**⚠️ VA and watts are not the same, and sizing is where this bites.** A UPS is rated in **VA (volt-amps, apparent power)** and in **watts (real power)**; the ratio between them is the **power factor**. A unit advertised at 1500 VA may support only around 900 W, so **the watt rating is the one that constrains what may be connected**.

**Batteries degrade.** Sealed lead-acid cells typically last three to five years, and capacity falls throughout that period — a UPS that comfortably held the load when installed may provide only a few minutes years later. **Runtime must be retested, not assumed.**

**Management connectivity is what makes graceful shutdown possible.** A USB or network link lets connected systems be signalled to shut down as the battery depletes; without it, a UPS only postpones an abrupt power loss rather than preventing one.

**A maintenance bypass** allows the UPS to be serviced or replaced without dropping the load.

### Power distribution unit (PDU)
A device that **distributes power to the equipment in a rack**. Available as a 1U horizontal unit or, more commonly in data centres, as a **0U vertical strip** mounted in the rack's rear channel so it consumes no rack units.

| Type | Provides |
|---|---|
| **Basic** | Outlets only — a rack-mounted power strip |
| **Metered** | Aggregate current display, so circuit capacity can be observed |
| **Switched** | **Remote per-outlet control** — a hung device can be power-cycled without a site visit |
| **Monitored / intelligent** | Per-outlet measurement, network reporting, and threshold alerts |

**A PDU contains no battery.** The chain runs **utility → UPS → PDU → equipment**.

**Redundant power design:** equipment with dual power supplies takes one cord from **PDU A** and one from **PDU B**, ideally fed from separate circuits and separate UPS units. Single-supply devices can be given the same protection with an **ATS (automatic transfer switch)**, which selects between two feeds.

**⚠️ Connecting both power supplies of a server to the same PDU removes the redundancy the second supply exists to provide** — a common oversight that is invisible until the PDU or its circuit fails.

### Power load
The **total electrical demand of the equipment supplied by a circuit**, expressed in watts or amps. It determines how much can safely be connected, and it is the figure that must be checked before adding anything to a rack.

**Circuit capacity:** a 20 A circuit at 120 V provides 2400 VA in theory.

**⚠️ The 80% rule.** Electrical code limits a **continuous load** — one drawing for three hours or more — to **80% of a circuit's rating**. A 20 A circuit is therefore limited to **16 A**, roughly **1920 W at 120 V**. Network and server equipment runs continuously by definition, so this derating always applies rather than being a safety margin one might choose to use.

**Redundant feeds derate further.** If two feeds are provisioned so that either can carry the entire load alone during a failure, **each must normally run at no more than about 40%** — otherwise the surviving feed is overloaded at the moment it is needed.

**Estimating vs. measuring:** summing nameplate ratings is conservative, because nameplate figures state maximum draw rather than typical draw. A **metered PDU** gives the real number, which is why metered units are the usual minimum specification in any rack that will grow.

**⚠️ Every watt consumed becomes a watt of heat.** Power load and cooling load are the same quantity expressed differently — roughly **3.41 BTU/hr per watt** — which is why the power budget and the cooling budget cannot be planned separately.

**When a circuit is overloaded, its breaker opens and everything on it stops**, including equipment that was well within its own limits. A UPS in the same condition transfers to bypass or shuts down.

### Voltage
The electrical potential supplied to equipment. **North America** distributes at **120 V** for standard outlets, with **208 V** and **240 V** used for higher-draw equipment; **most of the world uses 230 V**. Frequency is **60 Hz** in North America and **50 Hz** in most other regions.

**⚠️ Higher voltage is more efficient for the same power.** Because **P = V × I**, delivering a given wattage at a higher voltage requires proportionally **less current** — which reduces resistive loss in the conductors, permits smaller cable, and allows more equipment per circuit. Server power supplies are also typically **one to three percent more efficient** at 208–240 V than at 120 V. For these reasons data centres commonly distribute at **208 V** or three-phase.

**Most current equipment has auto-ranging power supplies** accepting 100–240 V at 50 or 60 Hz, so no configuration is required. **Older equipment may carry a manual voltage selector switch, and setting it incorrectly destroys the device on power-up.**

**Connector types encode the limits:** **NEMA 5-15** is the standard 120 V 15 A outlet, with locking variants such as **L5-30** used for higher-draw circuits. On PDUs and equipment, **IEC C13/C14** couplers are rated to 10 A and **C19/C20** to 16 A — the larger connector indicating the higher-draw device rather than being an arbitrary difference in shape.

**Power quality events worth naming:**

| Event | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Sag / brownout** | Voltage below nominal, sustained |
| **Surge / spike** | Voltage above nominal, brief |
| **Blackout** | Complete loss of supply |
| **Noise / harmonics** | Distortion superimposed on the waveform |

**Sags are far more frequent than blackouts**, which is what makes a line-interactive UPS's voltage regulation more valuable in practice than its battery.

## Environmental factors

### Temperature
Equipment converts essentially all consumed power into heat, so the cooling system must remove as much energy as the power system delivers.

**ASHRAE's recommended range for equipment intake air is 18–27 °C (64–81 °F)**, with wider allowable envelopes for short periods.

**⚠️ Measure at the intake, not at the room thermostat.** A room reading a comfortable ambient temperature can still be feeding recirculated exhaust to a device whose intake sits at 40 °C. Intake temperature is what the hardware experiences.

**Consequences of running hot:** processors throttle, reducing performance before anything is reported as failed; systems reach thermal shutdown; and **component life falls sharply**, with a common rule of thumb that every 10 °C above specification roughly halves the service life of electronic components. Failures therefore appear months later and are rarely attributed to the heat that caused them.

**Running too cold is wasteful rather than harmful**, but cold air holds less moisture and raises the risk of condensation when equipment or air warms.

### Humidity
**The recommended range is roughly 40–60% relative humidity.** Both extremes cause damage, in opposite ways.

| | **Too low** | **Too high** |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Charge accumulates in dry air | Moisture condenses on surfaces |
| Damage | **Electrostatic discharge (ESD)** | **Corrosion and short circuits** |
| Character | Often **latent** — the component fails weeks later | Progressive degradation of contacts and traces |

**ESD is the more insidious of the two**, because a discharge below the threshold of human perception can still damage a semiconductor, and the resulting failure is delayed and appears unrelated.

**⚠️ Condensation risk when moving equipment.** Hardware brought from a cold environment into a warm, humid room collects moisture on its surfaces. It must be allowed to reach room temperature before being powered on.

### Fire suppression
The objective is to extinguish a fire **without destroying the equipment or harming the occupants** — three requirements that conflict, which is why several systems exist.

| System | Suitability for equipment areas |
|---|---|
| **Clean agent** (FM-200, Novec 1230, Inergen) | **Best** — leaves no residue, safe for equipment and, at design concentration, for people |
| **Water mist** | **Suitable** — very fine droplets use a fraction of the water of sprinklers and cause far less damage; **safer for equipment than conventional sprinklers or foam** |
| **Pre-action sprinkler** | **Acceptable** — pipes stay dry and **two independent triggers** are required before water is released, so a single damaged head cannot flood the room |
| **Dry pipe sprinkler** | Reduces accidental discharge damage, at the cost of a delay |
| **Wet pipe sprinkler** | **⚠️ Poor** — water stands in the pipes, and a damaged head floods the room |
| **Foam** | **⚠️ Unsuitable** — residue destroys electronics |
| **CO₂** | **⚠️ Lethal to occupants** at extinguishing concentration; only for unoccupied spaces |
| **Halon** | **Phased out** under the Montreal Protocol as ozone-depleting; replaced by FM-200 and Novec |

**Class C** designates fires involving **energised electrical equipment**, and portable extinguishers in these spaces must carry that rating.

**Early detection matters more than the suppression method**, because a fire caught at the smouldering stage may not require discharge at all. **Aspirating smoke detection (VESDA)** continuously samples air and detects combustion products long before a conventional detector reacts.

**An EPO (emergency power off)** removes power so that suppression is not applied to energised equipment, and is mandated in some facilities.
