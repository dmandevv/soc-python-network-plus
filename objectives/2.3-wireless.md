# 2.3 — Given a scenario, select and configure wireless devices and technologies

## Channels

A **specific slice of frequency within a band that an access point transmits on.** A band such as 2.4 GHz is a wide range; channels divide it into numbered, individually selectable pieces so that nearby access points can avoid transmitting on top of each other.

**Numbering vs. actual frequency:** channel numbers are labels, not widths. In 2.4 GHz the numbered centres are spaced only **5 MHz apart** — channel 1 centres on 2412 MHz, channel 6 on 2437, channel 11 on 2462 — while each channel actually occupies about **20 MHz**. Adjacent numbers therefore overlap heavily.

**Why the choice matters — two different problems:**

| | **Co-channel interference** | **Adjacent-channel interference** |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Two APs on the **same** channel | Two APs on **partially overlapping** channels |
| Behaviour | They hear each other and **take turns** | They cannot decode each other, so it registers as **noise** |
| Result | Airtime is shared — throughput halves | Corrupted frames and retransmissions |
| Severity | Inefficient but orderly | **Worse** — actively destructive |

**The counterintuitive rule: partial overlap is worse than full overlap.** Wireless is half duplex and uses **CSMA/CA (Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance)**, so two APs on the identical channel can sense one another and politely alternate. Two APs on channels 1 and 3 cannot decode each other's transmissions, so neither defers — each simply raises the other's noise floor.

**Consequence:** deliberately reusing the same channel is a valid design. Staggering channels slightly is not.

### Channel width
**How much spectrum a single channel occupies** — 20, 40, 80, 160, or (in Wi-Fi 7) 320 MHz. Widths above 20 MHz are created by **bonding** adjacent 20 MHz channels into one wider channel.

**Each doubling roughly doubles the data rate**, because throughput is proportional to the bandwidth used.

**⚠️ Each doubling also costs three things:**

| Width | Relative rate | Noise floor | Non-overlapping channels (5 GHz, US) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 MHz | 1× | baseline | ~25 |
| 40 MHz | 2× | **+3 dB** | ~12 |
| 80 MHz | 4× | **+6 dB** | ~6 |
| 160 MHz | 8× | **+9 dB** | ~2 |

1. **Half the available channels** per doubling, making channel reuse harder in dense deployments.
2. **A higher noise floor** — a wider receiver collects proportionally more noise, so SNR falls ~3 dB per doubling. Lower SNR means lower modulation rates at the edge of coverage, so **wide channels shrink effective range**.
3. **A larger target for interference** — any interferer anywhere in the bonded range affects the whole channel, and if a sub-channel is busy the radio must fall back to a narrower width or wait.

**Practical selection:**
- **2.4 GHz — 20 MHz only.** The band holds three channels; 40 MHz makes overlap unavoidable.
- **5 GHz — 40 MHz in dense environments, 80 MHz at home.** 160 MHz consumes almost the whole band and requires DFS channels.
- **6 GHz — 160 MHz is practical**, since the band provides roughly 59 twenty-megahertz channels.

**The governing trade-off: width buys speed for one client and spends the capacity that would have served many.** In high-density design, narrower channels usually yield higher aggregate throughput.

### Non-overlapping channels
Channels whose frequency ranges **do not share any spectrum**, so two access points using them cannot interfere with each other regardless of proximity.

**2.4 GHz — only 1, 6, and 11.** Channel numbers are spaced 5 MHz apart while channels are 20 MHz wide, so five numbers of separation are required. The 83.5 MHz band fits exactly three.

**5 GHz — non-overlapping by design.** Channels are numbered on the same 5 MHz grid, but only every fourth number is allocated: 36, 40, 44, 48, and so on. Each is therefore **20 MHz apart from the next**, so any two listed channels at 20 MHz width are automatically non-overlapping.

| | **2.4 GHz** | **5 GHz** |
|---|---|---|
| Allocated numbers | Every number, 1–11 | Every fourth — 36, 40, 44, 48 … |
| Spacing between allocations | **5 MHz** | **20 MHz** |
| Can you pick a bad channel? | **Yes — anything but 1/6/11** | **No, at 20 MHz width** |

**That difference is why 5 GHz planning is easier.** In 2.4 GHz the channel list itself is a trap; in 5 GHz it is impossible to accidentally choose an overlapping channel unless channel bonding is enabled.

**Channel reuse:** with only three usable 2.4 GHz channels, large deployments **repeat** them in a honeycomb pattern so that no two adjacent cells share a channel. Same-channel access points are separated by enough distance or wall attenuation that they no longer hear each other. Neighbouring cells should still overlap by roughly **15–20%** so clients can roam without dropping.

**Roughly half of the 5 GHz channels require DFS** (Dynamic Frequency Selection) — see *regulatory impacts* below.

### Regulatory impacts
Wireless spectrum is **government-allocated**, so which channels are legal, and at what transmit power, varies by country. Access points carry a **regulatory domain / country code** setting that constrains the channels and power levels the radio will offer.

**Governing bodies:** FCC (United States), ISED (Canada), ETSI (Europe), with equivalents elsewhere.

**Examples of what changes across domains:**

| | United States | Europe |
|---|---|---|
| 2.4 GHz channels | 1–11 | **1–13** |
| 2.4 GHz power ceiling | Up to **36 dBm** EIRP | **20 dBm** EIRP |
| 5 GHz sub-bands | UNII-1/2A/2C/3 | Differing allocations |

Channel 14 is **Japan only**, and 802.11b only.

**⚠️ Setting the wrong country code is not a cosmetic error.** It can put the device outside legal limits and cause interference with licensed services such as radar, weather systems, and satellite links.

**EIRP (Effective Isotropic Radiated Power)** is what regulators actually cap — transmitter output **plus antenna gain, minus cable loss**. Fitting a higher-gain antenna therefore raises EIRP and can push a legal transmitter over the limit without changing any radio setting.

### 802.11h
The amendment that lets Wi-Fi share the 5 GHz band with **radar and satellite services**. Originally written to satisfy European regulators, now applied broadly. It defines two mechanisms:

**1. DFS (Dynamic Frequency Selection)** — the access point listens for radar signatures on its channel. On detection it must **vacate within 10 seconds** and may not return for a **30-minute non-occupancy period**. Before first using a DFS channel it must perform a **Channel Availability Check (CAC)** — 60 seconds of silent monitoring, or **10 minutes** on the weather-radar channels 120–128.

**2. TPC (Transmit Power Control)** — the access point transmits at the **lowest power that maintains the link**, rather than at maximum, limiting interference with protected services.

**Why it matters practically:** DFS channels roughly **double the usable 5 GHz spectrum**, but carry three costs.

- **Startup delay** — the CAC means a DFS channel is unavailable for the first 60 seconds after selection.
- **Mid-session channel changes** — a radar detection moves every client at once, interrupting traffic.
- **⚠️ Client support is inconsistent** — many low-cost IoT devices and older clients cannot use DFS channels at all. On a DFS channel, those devices **cannot see the SSID**, which presents as a device that "won't connect" with no error on the access point.

**Memory aid: 802.11h = DFS + TPC = coexisting with radar.**

## Frequency options

Wi-Fi operates in three unlicensed bands. The trade-off between them is governed by physics: **lower frequencies travel further and penetrate better, higher frequencies carry more spectrum and therefore more speed.**

| | **2.4 GHz** | **5 GHz** | **6 GHz** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Usable spectrum | **83.5 MHz** | ~500 MHz | **1200 MHz** |
| Non-overlapping 20 MHz channels | **3** | ~25 | ~59 |
| Range and wall penetration | **Best** | Moderate | **Worst** |
| Congestion | **Severe** | Moderate | **None** |
| Standards | 802.11b/g/n/ax | 802.11a/n/ac/ax | **802.11ax/be only** |
| DFS required | No | On roughly half | No |

**2.4 GHz** offers the longest range and the best penetration through walls, and is the only band many IoT devices support. It is also **shared with Bluetooth, microwave ovens, cordless phones, Zigbee, baby monitors, and wireless cameras**, and provides only three channels — so it is almost always the most congested band in any environment.

**5 GHz** trades range for capacity: roughly twenty-five channels instead of three, at the cost of shorter reach and weaker wall penetration. It is the default band for performance, with the caveat that about half its channels require DFS.

**6 GHz** (Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7) offers the most spectrum and, uniquely, **no legacy clients at all**. Because 802.11b/g/n/ac devices cannot operate there, the band carries none of the backwards-compatibility protection overhead that slows the older bands. Its range is the shortest of the three.

**The physics behind the range difference:** free-space path loss rises with frequency — doubling the frequency costs about **6 dB**. Moving from 2.4 GHz to 5 GHz costs roughly 6.4 dB before any obstacle is considered, and solid materials attenuate higher frequencies more severely as well.

**Practical division of labour:** IoT and distant devices on 2.4 GHz, laptops and phones on 5 GHz, high-throughput clients on 6 GHz where supported.

### Band steering
An access point feature that **encourages dual-band-capable clients onto the higher-capacity band**, typically 5 GHz, leaving 2.4 GHz for devices that support nothing else.

**It requires a single SSID broadcast on both radios.** If the bands are given separate names — `MyNet` and `MyNet-5G` — the client chooses for itself and band steering never comes into play.

**⚠️ The access point cannot force the decision.** Association is always initiated by the client, so band steering works only by influencing what the client believes is available:

- **Probe response suppression** — the access point ignores or delays the client's 2.4 GHz probe requests, so the client only discovers the 5 GHz radio. This is the most common method.
- **Association rejection** — refusing the 2.4 GHz association so the client retries elsewhere.
- **802.11v BSS Transition Management** — a standardised, cooperative *suggestion* that the client move. Well-behaved clients honour it; others ignore it.

**The classic failure — sticky clients:** a device associates on 5 GHz near the access point, then moves away. Its signal degrades badly, but because the connection has not actually dropped, the client refuses to fall back to 2.4 GHz. The result is a device holding a barely usable 5 GHz link while a strong 2.4 GHz signal sits unused. Roaming aggressiveness is a client-side setting, so this is generally not fixable from the access point.

**The second failure — IoT devices:** aggressive probe suppression, or a shared dual-band SSID, can prevent 2.4 GHz-only devices from joining or completing setup. The common remedy is a **separate 2.4 GHz-only SSID for IoT**, which also allows that traffic to be mapped to its own VLAN.

**Distinguish it from client steering**, which moves clients **between access points** to balance load. Band steering moves a client **between radios on the same access point**.

## Service set identifier (SSID)

The **network name** — up to 32 characters, case sensitive — advertised in the access point's beacon frames and shown to users in a list of available networks. It is a **label, not an address**, and multiple access points can share one.

### Basic service set identifier (BSSID)
The **MAC address of the access point radio serving a particular network** — a 48-bit value that uniquely identifies one radio broadcasting one SSID.

**The detail worth remembering: an access point has one BSSID per SSID per radio.** An access point broadcasting three SSIDs across a 2.4 GHz and a 5 GHz radio presents **six BSSIDs**. Each is a separate **BSS (Basic Service Set)** — one radio and the clients associated to it.

### Extended service set identifier (ESSID)
The SSID **shared across multiple access points** joined by a common distribution system (the wired network), forming an **ESS (Extended Service Set)** that clients can roam across as a single logical network.

In everyday use *ESSID* and *SSID* are used interchangeably; the distinction the exam draws is that an ESSID names a set of access points rather than one.

| Term | What it identifies | Form |
|---|---|---|
| **SSID** | The network **name** | Text, up to 32 characters |
| **BSSID** | One **radio** serving that network | **MAC address** |
| **ESSID** | The name shared across **many access points** | Text — same value on each |

**How they interact during roaming:** a client moving between access points keeps the same **SSID/ESSID** and changes **BSSID**. The name never changes; the address does.

**Its diagnostic value:** because the BSSID identifies a specific radio, reading it tells you exactly which access point a client is attached to — `netsh wlan show interfaces` on Windows, `iw dev wlan0 link` on Linux. This is the standard way to confirm a sticky client is holding on to a distant access point.

**⚠️ Disabling SSID broadcast is not a security control.** It removes the name from beacon frames only; the SSID still appears in probe requests, association frames, and probe responses, so any passive capture recovers it in seconds. It also breaks or degrades connection behaviour on some clients, and causes them to broadcast the network name while searching for it — actively leaking it. Treat it as obscurity, not protection.

## Network types

| Type | Central device | Clients talk via | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Infrastructure** | Access point | **The access point** | Essentially all production Wi-Fi |
| **Ad hoc** | None | **Each other, directly** | Temporary peer-to-peer links |
| **Mesh** | Access points relaying wirelessly | Nearest node, then hop to the gateway | Coverage where cable cannot run |
| **Point to point** | Two endpoints only | A dedicated directional link | Building-to-building bridging |

### Infrastructure
The standard mode, in which clients associate to an **access point** that bridges them to a wired **distribution system**. This forms a **BSS (Basic Service Set)**.

**The property worth noting: clients never communicate directly.** Two devices on the same access point exchange frames as client → access point → client, so intra-cell traffic consumes **twice the airtime** of the same transfer over a wire. It is also what makes **client isolation** possible — the access point can simply decline to forward between them.

### Ad hoc
A peer-to-peer wireless network with **no access point**, formally an **IBSS (Independent Basic Service Set)**. Devices communicate directly with one another.

**Limitations:** no distribution system, so no wired connectivity or roaming; no central point at which to apply security or QoS policy; poor scaling, since every device must be in range of every other. Suited to quick temporary links rather than deployment.

### Mesh
Access points that connect to **each other wirelessly** rather than each requiring a wired uplink. One or more **root nodes** carry a wired connection to the network; the remaining nodes relay traffic toward them. Standardised as **802.11s**.

**Its strengths are self-configuration and self-healing** — nodes discover neighbours automatically and reroute around a failed node without manual intervention. Its value is coverage where cabling is impractical.

**⚠️ Its cost is throughput per hop.** If a node uses the same radio for client access and for backhaul, that radio must receive a frame and then retransmit it, **roughly halving throughput at each hop**. Tri-band mesh systems avoid this by dedicating a separate radio to backhaul.

### Point to point
A dedicated wireless link between **exactly two locations**, typically using **directional antennas** at each end. Common for bridging buildings across a distance where cable or fibre is impractical.

**Requirements:** line of sight, careful antenna alignment, and high antenna gain. The related **point-to-multipoint** arrangement uses one central hub antenna serving several directional remotes.

## Encryption

**Lineage:** WEP (broken, RC4, trivially cracked) → WPA (TKIP, a stopgap for WEP-era hardware) → **WPA2** (AES-CCMP) → **WPA3** (SAE). Only the last two are current.

### Wi-Fi Protected Access 2 (WPA2)
The implementation of **802.11i**, using **CCMP** (Counter Mode with CBC-MAC Protocol) with **AES-128** for encryption and integrity. It has been the default for roughly two decades.

**How keys are established:** a **4-way handshake** derives a per-session key (the PTK) from the shared master key (the PMK). In Personal mode the PMK comes directly from the passphrase.

**⚠️ Its central weakness is the offline dictionary attack.** An attacker captures the 4-way handshake — or forces one by sending a **forged deauthentication frame** to a connected client — and then brute-forces the passphrase **offline**, at whatever speed their hardware allows. Nothing rate-limits the guessing because the network is no longer involved.

**Its other weaknesses:**
- **No forward secrecy** — traffic captured today can be decrypted later if the passphrase is ever learned.
- **A shared key means shared visibility** — every device on a Personal network uses the same passphrase, so any member who captures a neighbour's handshake can decrypt that neighbour's traffic.
- **Management frames are unprotected by default**, which is what makes the forced-deauthentication step possible.
- **KRACK** (2017) forced nonce reuse in the handshake; patched in software.

### WPA3
Introduced in 2018. It retains AES but replaces the way keys are established.

**Its defining change is SAE (Simultaneous Authentication of Equals)**, also called the Dragonfly handshake — a **PAKE (Password Authenticated Key Exchange)**.

**⚠️ SAE is not a pre-shared key exchange.** Both sides prove knowledge of the password through a Diffie-Hellman-style exchange in which **the password itself is never transmitted** and is never used directly as the session key.

Two consequences follow:

- **Offline dictionary attacks stop working.** Each password guess requires a **fresh live exchange with the access point**, so guessing is rate-limited by the network rather than by the attacker's hardware.
- **Forward secrecy is provided.** Each session derives an independent key, so capturing traffic today and learning the password tomorrow yields nothing.

**PMF (Protected Management Frames, 802.11w) is mandatory in WPA3**, where WPA2 left it optional. This authenticates management frames and defeats the forged-deauthentication attack outright.

| | **WPA2** | **WPA3** |
|---|---|---|
| Personal key exchange | Passphrase → PMK → 4-way handshake | **SAE** |
| Offline dictionary attack | **Vulnerable** | **Resistant** |
| Forward secrecy | **No** | **Yes** |
| Protected management frames | Optional | **Mandatory** |
| Cipher | CCMP / AES-128 | CCMP-128; **GCMP-256** in Enterprise 192-bit mode |

**Related additions:** **OWE (Opportunistic Wireless Encryption)**, marketed as Wi-Fi Enhanced Open, encrypts traffic on **open networks with no password** — closing the gap where guest and public Wi-Fi transmitted in cleartext.

**⚠️ Transition mode** runs WPA2 and WPA3 on one SSID so older clients can still connect. It is a practical necessity during migration, but a client can be **downgraded to WPA2**, which reinstates every WPA2 weakness. WPA3-only is the goal once client support allows it.

## Guest networks

A separate SSID for visitors that provides **internet access without access to the internal network**.

**⚠️ The SSID itself isolates nothing.** A guest network is the combination of three controls:

1. **A dedicated SSID** mapped to **its own VLAN**, separating guest traffic at Layer 2
2. **Firewall rules** permitting outbound internet while denying every internal subnet
3. **Client isolation** (also called AP or station isolation), preventing guests from reaching *each other*

**Client isolation works because of infrastructure mode.** Since every frame between two associated clients must pass through the access point, the access point can simply decline to forward it. There is no path around that decision.

**Commonly added:** bandwidth rate limiting so guests cannot consume the whole uplink, and time-limited or rotating credentials.

### Captive portals
A page presented to a newly connected client that requires **acceptance of terms, login, registration, or payment before network access is granted**.

**How the interception works:**
1. The client associates and receives an address by DHCP as normal
2. The gateway places that client in an **unauthenticated state** — all traffic is blocked except DNS and the portal itself
3. The client's first plain **HTTP** request is answered with a **redirect** to the portal
4. On acceptance, the gateway adds the client's MAC and IP to its permitted list

**⚠️ HTTPS defeats interception.** A portal cannot redirect an HTTPS request without presenting a certificate for a domain it does not own, which produces a browser security error rather than a login page.

**This is why operating systems probe deliberately.** Each vendor requests a known plain-HTTP URL on joining a network — `captive.apple.com`, `connectivitycheck.gstatic.com`, `msftconnecttest.com` — and treats an unexpected response as evidence of a portal, which is what triggers the sign-in window. **RFC 8910** provides a cleaner alternative, advertising the portal URL through a DHCP option rather than by interception.

**Security limitations:**
- Authorisation is typically bound to **MAC address**, which is **spoofable** — an attacker can clone an authorised client's address and inherit its access
- Traffic before authentication is **unencrypted**, and a portal on an open SSID leaves it unencrypted afterwards too
- A portal controls **access**, not confidentiality — it is not a substitute for encryption

## Authentication — PSK vs. Enterprise

Both WPA2 and WPA3 offer two authentication modes. They differ in **whether every device shares one secret or each user has their own**.

### Pre-shared key (PSK) — Personal mode
A **single passphrase used by every device** on the network. The passphrase, combined with the SSID as a salt, is stretched through **PBKDF2** to produce the master key.

**Its advantage is that it requires no infrastructure** — configure the passphrase on the access point and on each client, and the network works.

**⚠️ Its weaknesses are all consequences of the secret being shared:**
- **No per-user identity.** Logs show which device connected, never which person.
- **No individual revocation.** Removing one person's access means changing the passphrase on **every** device.
- **No isolation between members.** Any device holding the passphrase can derive another device's session key from a captured handshake.
- **Shared secrets spread** — read aloud to visitors, written down, retained on old devices indefinitely.

(**WPA3-Personal replaces the key exchange with SAE**, which removes the offline-cracking exposure, but the secret is still shared and the management weaknesses above remain.)

### Enterprise mode
Authentication through **802.1X**, using **EAP (Extensible Authentication Protocol)** against a central **RADIUS (Remote Authentication Dial-In User Service)** server. Every user holds **unique credentials**.

**Three roles define the exchange:**

| Role | Device | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Supplicant** | The client | Presents credentials |
| **Authenticator** | Access point or switch | **Relays only — makes no decision** |
| **Authentication server** | RADIUS | **Decides** accept or reject |

The supplicant talks to the authenticator using **EAPOL** (EAP over LAN); the authenticator talks to the server using **RADIUS**, on **UDP 1812** for authentication and **1813** for accounting.

**What unique credentials buy:**
- **Per-user identity and accounting** in logs
- **Individual revocation** — disable one account, nothing else changes
- **A unique master key per user per session**, so members cannot decrypt one another's traffic
- **Dynamic VLAN assignment** — RADIUS can return a VLAN attribute, placing each user in the correct segment automatically

**Common EAP methods:**

| Method | Client credential | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **EAP-TLS** | **Certificate** | Strongest; no passwords; requires a PKI |
| **PEAP** | Username/password | Inside a TLS tunnel using a server certificate; most widely deployed |
| **EAP-TTLS** | Username/password | Similar to PEAP |

| | **PSK / Personal** | **Enterprise** |
|---|---|---|
| Secret | **One, shared by all** | **Unique per user** |
| Infrastructure required | None | **RADIUS server** |
| Revoke one user | Change the passphrase everywhere | **Disable one account** |
| Identity in logs | Device only | **User** |
| Per-user VLAN assignment | Not possible | **Supported** |
| Suited to | Home, small office | Business and campus |

**Note that 802.1X is the same mechanism used on wired switch ports** — the wireless case simply substitutes an access point for a switch as the authenticator.

## Antennas

**⚠️ An antenna does not amplify.** It **redistributes** a fixed amount of transmitted energy. Gain describes how tightly that energy is focused, so any gain in one direction is taken from another — a high-gain antenna reaches further only because it covers less area.

**Gain is measured in dBi** — decibels relative to an **isotropic radiator**, a theoretical point source radiating equally in every direction.

### Omnidirectional
Radiates **360° horizontally**, producing a doughnut-shaped pattern centred on the antenna. Typical gain is **2–6 dBi**. This is the standard antenna on an access point, appropriate when clients are distributed all around it.

**⚠️ Raising an omnidirectional antenna's gain flattens the doughnut.** Horizontal reach increases and vertical coverage shrinks. A high-gain omnidirectional antenna mounted on a ceiling can therefore create a **dead zone directly beneath it**, and is a poor choice for multi-storey coverage.

### Directional
Focuses energy into a **beam**, giving far greater range over a narrower area. Typical gain is **8–30 dBi**.

| Type | Gain | Pattern | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Patch / panel** | 6–15 dBi | Wide beam, one direction | Wall mount, corridors, aiming coverage inward |
| **Yagi** | 10–20 dBi | Narrow beam | Medium-range directional links |
| **Parabolic dish / grid** | 20–30+ dBi | **Very narrow** | Long-range point to point |

**Beamwidth is the inverse of gain.** Beamwidth is the angle within which the signal remains within 3 dB of its peak; the more gain an antenna has, the narrower that angle becomes. A high-gain dish may have a beamwidth of only a few degrees, making **alignment critical** — pole flex or wind can be enough to degrade the link.

| | **Omnidirectional** | **Directional** |
|---|---|---|
| Coverage | 360° horizontal | A focused beam |
| Gain | Low (2–6 dBi) | High (8–30 dBi) |
| Range | Shorter, all around | **Much longer, one direction** |
| Alignment | Not required | **Critical** |
| Use | General client coverage | **Point to point**, corridors, distant clients |

**A security application:** directional antennas reduce signal spill beyond a building's boundary, shrinking the area from which the network can be attacked. Aiming coverage inward is a legitimate physical control.

**⚠️ Antenna gain counts toward the regulatory EIRP limit.** Replacing a 3 dBi omnidirectional antenna with a 15 dBi dish adds 12 dB of EIRP without changing any transmitter setting, and can place an otherwise compliant device outside its legal ceiling.

**Polarisation must match at both ends** — a vertically polarised antenna paired with a horizontally polarised one loses roughly 20 dB, which is enough to prevent a link from forming at all.

### ⚠️ Why raising transmit power backfires
Regulatory limits aside, increasing an access point's transmit power usually **degrades** the network rather than extending it.

**The link is bidirectional, but only one end got louder.** A phone or laptop transmits at roughly **15 dBm** and that cannot be raised. Increasing the access point to 30 dBm therefore creates an **asymmetric link**: the client hears the beacon clearly from far away and associates, but its own transmissions never reach the access point. The result is a device that shows a strong signal and full bars while passing no traffic — a worse failure than not connecting at all.

**Larger cells also reduce capacity.** A wider coverage area places more clients in one collision domain contending for the same airtime, and increases co-channel interference with neighbouring access points on the same channel.

**Driving a radio beyond its designed output distorts the signal.** Amplifier non-linearity causes spectral regrowth into adjacent channels and raises error vector magnitude, which forces rate adaptation **down** — transmitting harder produces a slower link.

**The design rule is the opposite of the intuition: in dense deployments, transmit power is reduced**, so cells are small, clients roam sooner, and channels can be reused more often. Coverage problems are solved by adding access points, not by amplifying existing ones.

## Autonomous vs. lightweight AP

The distinction is **where the intelligence lives** — in each access point, or in a central controller.

### Autonomous
A **self-contained** access point, also called *fat*, *thick*, or *standalone*. It holds its own configuration, SSIDs, security policy, and radio settings, and is administered through its own web interface or CLI. No controller is required.

**Appropriate for a handful of access points.** Beyond that, its limitations compound: every configuration change must be repeated on every device; there is no coordinated channel or power assignment; and nothing correlates events across access points for monitoring or rogue detection.

### Lightweight
A **thin** access point that depends on a **WLC (Wireless LAN Controller)** for configuration and radio management. It is provisioned automatically — on connection it discovers the controller and downloads its configuration, so replacing a failed unit requires no manual setup.

**The controller provides what individual access points cannot:**
- **RRM (Radio Resource Management)** — automatic channel and transmit power assignment across the whole deployment, adjusted as conditions change
- **Coordinated fast roaming** between access points
- **Rogue access point detection** by correlating what every access point hears
- **Centralised configuration, firmware distribution, and monitoring**

**The protocol is CAPWAP** (Control And Provisioning of Wireless Access Points), the standardised successor to Cisco's LWAPP, using **UDP 5246** for control and **5247** for data.

**Split-MAC architecture:** time-critical functions — beacons, acknowledgements, encryption — remain on the access point, because they must happen in microseconds. Management functions move to the controller.

**Two forwarding models:**

| Model | Client data path | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Centralised (tunnelled)** | Tunnelled to the controller, then out | Uniform policy, but the controller is a bottleneck and traffic takes an indirect path |
| **Local / distributed** | Bridged onto the local VLAN at the access point | Efficient paths, suited to branch sites; policy is less centralised |

**⚠️ The controller is a single point of failure** in a centralised design. Mitigations include controller high-availability pairs and local-forwarding modes that allow access points to keep serving clients when the controller is unreachable.

| | **Autonomous** | **Lightweight** |
|---|---|---|
| Configuration | **Per device** | **Central, automatic** |
| Controller | Not required | **Required** |
| Channel and power planning | Manual | **Automatic (RRM)** |
| Replacing a unit | Reconfigure by hand | **Plug in — it self-provisions** |
| Scales to | A few access points | **Hundreds** |
| Failure risk | Independent units | **Controller dependency** |

**Note that "controller" no longer implies a hardware appliance.** It may be a virtual machine, a cloud service, or software embedded in one of the access points itself — and some systems use the controller only for management, leaving each access point able to run independently if it becomes unreachable.

### Why acknowledgements are microsecond-critical

**Wi-Fi has no collision detection.** A radio cannot listen while it transmits, so unlike wired Ethernet's CSMA/CD it can never observe a collision directly. The only evidence that a frame arrived is an **explicit Layer 2 acknowledgement**, so every unicast frame is individually acknowledged.

**The acknowledgement is scheduled by SIFS (Short Interframe Space)** — the receiver waits one SIFS after the frame ends, then transmits. SIFS is **16 µs** for OFDM and 10 µs for the older DSSS rates, sized to cover the receiver's hardware turnaround: decoding the frame, verifying the FCS, and switching the radio from receive to transmit.

**Interframe spaces create a priority hierarchy** (5 GHz OFDM values):

| Interval | Length | Used by |
|---|---|---|
| **SIFS** | **16 µs** | ACK, CTS, block ACK |
| PIFS | 25 µs | Priority access |
| **DIFS** | **34 µs** | **Ordinary data transmission** |

**Because SIFS is shorter than DIFS, the acknowledgement always wins the medium.** This is not a rule granting the ACK priority — it is a timing gap so short that no other station has legally begun transmitting yet.

**⚠️ A late acknowledgement therefore causes a collision, not merely a delay.** If the gap stretches past DIFS, another station lawfully starts transmitting; the ACK then collides with it, both frames are lost, and the original sender retransmits a frame that had in fact been received.

**This is what makes the controller architecturally impossible in that path.** A round trip to a controller across a switched network is hundreds of microseconds at best, and tens of milliseconds to a cloud-hosted one — one to three orders of magnitude beyond a 16 µs budget. The acknowledgement is generated in the access point's MAC hardware, without involving even its own CPU, which is precisely why **split-MAC leaves acknowledgements, beacons, and encryption on the access point**.

**Block acknowledgement** (802.11n onward) reduces this overhead: frames are aggregated into an A-MPDU and a single block ACK confirms many subframes at once, rather than spending a SIFS gap and an ACK frame on each.

**⚠️ Alignment and polarisation are separate faults.** Alignment is **where the antenna points**; polarisation is **how it is rotated about that axis**. An antenna can be aimed perfectly and still be rotated 90° from its partner. The diagnostic signature is a link that establishes but sits roughly **20 dB below its calculated signal** with clear line of sight — cross-polarisation discrimination is typically 20–30 dB, so that figure identifies the fault the way late collisions identify a duplex mismatch.
