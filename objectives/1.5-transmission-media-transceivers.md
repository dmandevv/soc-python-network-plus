# 1.5 — Compare and contrast transmission media and transceivers

## Wireless

### 802.11 standards

| Standard | Wi-Fi name | Band(s) | Max theoretical | Key addition |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **802.11a** | — | 5 GHz | 54 Mbps | OFDM; incompatible with b |
| **802.11b** | — | 2.4 GHz | 11 Mbps | DSSS; longer range, slower |
| **802.11g** | — | 2.4 GHz | 54 Mbps | OFDM at 2.4 GHz; backward compatible with b |
| **802.11n** | **Wi-Fi 4** | 2.4 + 5 GHz | 600 Mbps | **MIMO**, 40 MHz channel bonding |
| **802.11ac** | **Wi-Fi 5** | **5 GHz only** | ~6.9 Gbps | **MU-MIMO** (downlink), 80/160 MHz |
| **802.11ax** | **Wi-Fi 6 / 6E** | 2.4 + 5 + **6 GHz** | ~9.6 Gbps | **OFDMA**, MU-MIMO both directions, BSS coloring |
| **802.11be** | **Wi-Fi 7** | 2.4 + 5 + 6 GHz | ~46 Gbps | 320 MHz channels, **MLO**, 4096-QAM |

**The two most-tested facts:** 802.11ac is **5 GHz only** (no 2.4 GHz support at all), and 6 GHz arrived with **802.11ax** — specifically the "6E" designation.

**Technologies that separate the generations:**

- **MIMO** (Multiple Input Multiple Output) — several antennas transmitting parallel spatial streams to one device, multiplying throughput.
- **MU-MIMO** (Multi-User MIMO) — serves several devices simultaneously rather than one at a time. Downlink only in 802.11ac; both directions in 802.11ax.
- **OFDMA** (Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiple Access) — subdivides a channel so multiple devices share it in the same transmission. This is why Wi-Fi 6 improves *dense* environments far more than it improves single-device speed.
- **Channel bonding** — combining adjacent 20 MHz channels into wider ones (40/80/160/320 MHz). More throughput, fewer non-overlapping channels available.

**Reality check:** these are theoretical maximums assuming maximum spatial streams, widest channels, and ideal conditions. Real throughput is a fraction of them, and the medium is shared half-duplex, so the figure is a ceiling divided across all associated clients.

### Cellular
Wide-area wireless delivered by carrier networks of licensed-spectrum towers, with **handoff** between cells as a device moves. Because the spectrum is licensed rather than shared, interference is controlled — the opposite of Wi-Fi's unlicensed free-for-all.

| Generation | Typical real-world speed | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 3G | 1–5 Mbps | Largely decommissioned |
| **4G LTE** | 10–50 Mbps | Still the coverage workhorse |
| **5G** | 50 Mbps – 1 Gbps+ | Sub-6 GHz for range; **mmWave** for extreme speed over very short distances |

**Network uses:** WAN failover for a branch office (a cellular modem takes over if the primary circuit drops), connectivity where wired service does not reach, IoT sensors, and temporary sites. Costs are usage-based and latency is higher than wired, so it is normally a backup or last-resort link rather than a primary one.

### Satellite
Connectivity via orbiting satellites rather than terrestrial infrastructure — the option of last resort where nothing else reaches, and the only one whose defining weakness is **latency imposed by physics**.

| Orbit | Altitude | One-way latency | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GEO** (geostationary) | ~35,786 km | **~250 ms** each way (~500+ ms round trip) | HughesNet, Viasat |
| **LEO** (low Earth orbit) | ~550 km | **~20–40 ms** round trip | Starlink |

**Why GEO latency cannot be engineered away:** the signal travels to orbit and back at the speed of light. Distance sets the floor. Faster hardware and more bandwidth change nothing.

**What that breaks:** VoIP, video calls, remote desktop, online gaming, and anything else interactive. TCP also suffers, since its throughput depends on round-trip acknowledgements.

**LEO trades altitude for complexity** — satellites are far closer so latency is usable, but they move fast relative to the ground, requiring large constellations and tracking antennas that hand off between satellites continuously.

**Other constraints:** rain fade (heavy precipitation attenuates the signal), the need for clear line of sight to the sky, and data caps on most plans.

## Wired

### 802.3 standards
The IEEE family defining Ethernet. Names decode as **speed – transmission method – medium**: `1000BASE-T` is 1000 Mbps, BASEband signalling, Twisted pair.

| Standard | Speed | Medium | Max distance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10BASE-T | 10 Mbps | Cat3+ copper | 100 m |
| 100BASE-TX | 100 Mbps | Cat5+ copper | 100 m |
| **1000BASE-T** | 1 Gbps | **Cat5e+** copper | **100 m** |
| 1000BASE-SX / LX | 1 Gbps | Multimode / single-mode fiber | 550 m / 5 km |
| **10GBASE-T** | 10 Gbps | **Cat6a+** copper | 100 m (Cat6 only 55 m) |
| 10GBASE-SR / LR | 10 Gbps | Multimode / single-mode fiber | 400 m / 10 km |
| **10GBASE-CR** | 10 Gbps | **Twinax DAC** | ~7 m |
| 40GBASE / 100GBASE | 40 / 100 Gbps | Fiber, QSFP | Varies |

**The 100 m limit on copper is the number to know** — it covers cable *and* patch leads, and it's a signal-integrity boundary, not a suggestion. Exceeding it causes attenuation and intermittent errors rather than a clean failure.

**Suffix decoder** — the letters after BASE come in two kinds, **medium** then **encoding**:

| Letter | Kind | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **T** | Medium | **T**wisted pair copper |
| **S** | Medium | **S**hort wavelength — multimode fiber |
| **L** | Medium | **L**ong wavelength — single-mode fiber |
| **E** | Medium | **E**xtended reach single-mode |
| **C** | Medium | **C**opper twinax (DAC) |
| **X** | Encoding | 8B/10B block encoding |
| **R** | Encoding | **64B/66B** block encoding |
| **digit** | Lanes | Number of parallel lanes |

So `10GBASE-SR` = short-wavelength multimode, 64B/66B. `1000BASE-SX` = same medium, older 8B/10B encoding. **`40GBASE-SR4`** = multimode, 64B/66B, **4 lanes** — which is why it needs 8 strands and an MPO connector rather than a duplex LC.

### Single-mode vs. multimode fiber

| | **Single-mode (SMF)** | **Multimode (MMF)** |
|---|---|---|
| Core diameter | **~9 µm** | **50 or 62.5 µm** |
| Light path | One straight path | Many paths bouncing at different angles |
| Light source | **Laser** | **LED / VCSEL** |
| Distance | **Kilometres** (10–100+ km) | **Hundreds of metres** |
| Cable cost | Lower | Higher |
| Transceiver cost | **Higher** | Lower |
| Jacket colour | **Yellow** | **Orange / aqua / violet** |
| Typical use | WAN, carrier, campus backbone | Inside a building or data centre |

**Why the core size decides everything:** a wide multimode core lets light enter at many angles, so rays take paths of differing length and arrive at slightly different times. That spreading — **modal dispersion** — blurs pulses together and limits distance. Single-mode's narrow core admits essentially one path, so there is nothing to disperse.

**The cost inversion is the tested detail:** single-mode *cable* is cheaper, but its precision lasers make the *transceivers* more expensive. Multimode is the reverse. Short runs therefore favour multimode overall, long runs single-mode.

**MMF grades:** OM1/OM2 (older, 62.5/50 µm), **OM3 (aqua)** and **OM4 (aqua/violet)** are laser-optimised for 10G+, **OM5 (lime green)** adds wavelength multiplexing.

### Direct attach copper (DAC) / twinaxial cable
A short, fixed-length **copper** assembly with the transceiver connectors (SFP+/QSFP) **permanently attached to both ends** — you buy the cable and its optics as one non-separable unit. The conductor inside is **twinaxial**: two signal wires in a shared shield, unlike coax's single conductor.

**Why it exists:** for the short hops inside a rack — switch to server, switch to switch — it is far cheaper than two optical transceivers plus a fiber patch lead, draws less power, and adds less latency because there is no electrical-to-optical conversion at either end.

**Its limit is distance: roughly 7 metres** (10GBASE-CR). Beyond that, copper at these speeds runs out and you must move to fiber.

**Passive vs. active:** passive DACs are plain shielded cable (~5 m); active DACs add signal-conditioning electronics to reach a bit further.

**Compatibility caveat:** many switch vendors validate DACs against a whitelist and may refuse third-party cables — a real operational gotcha, not a theoretical one.

### Coaxial cable
A **single** centre conductor surrounded by insulation, a braided shield, and an outer jacket — the shield sharing the same axis as the conductor, hence "co-axial." The shield gives it strong resistance to EMI, which is why it survived where early twisted pair could not.

| Type | Impedance | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **RG-6** | 75 Ω | **Cable internet and TV** — the one in homes today |
| RG-59 | 75 Ω | Legacy CCTV, short video runs |
| RG-58 | 50 Ω | Obsolete 10BASE2 "thinnet" Ethernet |

**Connectors:** **F-type** (screw-on, cable TV/internet) and **BNC** (bayonet twist-lock, legacy Ethernet and video) — both appear later in this objective.

**Where you meet it now:** the drop from the street to a cable modem. Your XB6's WAN side is RG-6 coax carrying **DOCSIS**; everything past the modem is Ethernet. Coax is not used for LAN wiring any more — 10BASE2 was a shared bus where a single break took down the entire segment, which is precisely the failure mode structured twisted-pair cabling eliminated.

### Cable speeds (copper categories)

| Category | Speed | Distance | Bandwidth | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cat3 | 10 Mbps | 100 m | 16 MHz | Obsolete; telephone |
| Cat5 | 100 Mbps | 100 m | 100 MHz | **Superseded — not valid for gigabit** |
| **Cat5e** | **1 Gbps** | 100 m | 100 MHz | "e" = enhanced; reduced crosstalk |
| **Cat6** | 1 Gbps / **10 Gbps** | 100 m / **55 m** | 250 MHz | 10G only over the shorter distance |
| **Cat6a** | **10 Gbps** | **100 m** | 500 MHz | "a" = augmented; handles alien crosstalk |
| Cat7 | 10 Gbps | 100 m | 600 MHz | Shielded; non-standard connector, rarely used |
| Cat8 | 25/40 Gbps | **30 m** | 2000 MHz | Data-centre top-of-rack only |

**The exam traps:**
- **Cat5 is not gigabit-rated** — Cat5**e** is. Older material and job sites use "Cat5" loosely, but for the exam the distinction is strict.
- **Cat6 does 10 Gbps at only 55 m**; Cat6a reaches the full 100 m. The difference is **alien crosstalk** — interference from *adjacent cables*, which Cat6a's construction addresses.
- **Cat8 is limited to 30 m**, so it is a rack-scale cable, not a building one.

**STP vs. UTP:** shielded twisted pair adds foil or braid against EMI, and **must be properly grounded** — an ungrounded shield acts as an antenna and makes interference worse. UTP is the default for ordinary installs.

### Plenum vs. non-plenum cable
A **fire-safety** distinction about the cable jacket, not a performance one. Both carry identical data.

A **plenum** is any building space used for air circulation — most commonly the void above a suspended ceiling or below a raised floor, where return air flows back to the HVAC system.

| | **Plenum (CMP)** | **Non-plenum (CMR / riser, PVC)** |
|---|---|---|
| Jacket | Fluoropolymer (FEP) | PVC |
| When burned | Low smoke, low toxicity, flame-resistant | **Dense toxic smoke** |
| Required in | **Air-handling spaces** | Walls, conduit, open runs |
| Cost | ~2× | Baseline |

**Why it matters:** ordinary PVC releases hydrogen chloride and dense black smoke when it burns. In a plenum, the HVAC system then distributes that smoke throughout the building — turning a contained fire into a building-wide inhalation hazard. **Smoke inhalation, not flame, is what kills people in structure fires.**

**This is a legal requirement**, not best practice — building codes (NFPA 70 / the National Electrical Code) mandate plenum-rated cable in air-handling spaces. Failing an inspection means pulling and replacing every run.

**Rating hierarchy:** CMP (plenum) > CMR (riser, for vertical shafts between floors) > CM/CMG (general) > CMX (residential). **Higher ratings can substitute for lower ones, never the reverse.**

## Transceivers

A transceiver (**trans**mitter + re**ceiver**) converts between a device's internal electrical signalling and the medium it sends over — typically electrical to optical for fiber. Modular transceivers let one switch port support copper, multimode, or single-mode simply by changing the module.

### Protocol — Ethernet vs. Fibre Channel

The same physical form factor can carry different protocols, and the module must match what the network runs.

| | **Ethernet** | **Fibre Channel (FC)** |
|---|---|---|
| Carries | LAN/WAN data traffic | **Storage** traffic (SCSI commands) |
| Network | Ordinary switched network | Dedicated SAN fabric |
| Loss tolerance | Lossy — upper layers recover | **Lossless** by design; storage cannot tolerate drops |
| Speeds | 1/10/25/40/100 GbE | 8/16/32/64 GFC |
| Hardware | Standard NICs and switches | **HBAs** and FC switches |

**Why FC exists as a separate protocol:** storage traffic behaves unlike network traffic. A dropped packet in a web request is retransmitted and nobody notices; a dropped block write is data corruption. FC provides lossless delivery with credit-based flow control at the fabric level, rather than relying on retransmission.

**FCoE (Fibre Channel over Ethernet)** carries FC frames over Ethernet, converging both onto one fabric — cheaper, but requires lossless Ethernet (DCB) to work correctly.

**Practical warning:** an SFP+ module built for Fibre Channel will not work in an Ethernet switch port even though it fits perfectly. Form factor is about the *slot*; protocol is about what the module speaks.

### Form factors — SFP and QSFP

| Form factor | Lanes | Typical speed | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SFP** | 1 | 1 Gbps | The original small form-factor pluggable |
| **SFP+** | 1 | **10 Gbps** | Same physical size as SFP; most common today |
| SFP28 | 1 | 25 Gbps | |
| **QSFP+** | **4** | **40 Gbps** | "**Q**uad" — four lanes of 10 Gbps |
| QSFP28 | 4 | 100 Gbps | Four lanes of 25 Gbps |

**The Q is literal: QSFP is four SFP lanes in one module.** That is why 4 × 10 = 40 Gbps and 4 × 25 = 100 Gbps. A QSFP is physically larger than an SFP and the slots are not interchangeable.

**Breakout cables** exploit the lane structure — one QSFP+ port can split into four separate 10 Gbps SFP+ links, which is how a data-centre spine switch fans out to multiple top-of-rack switches from a single port.

**Backward compatibility:** SFP+ cages generally accept 1 Gbps SFP modules, so a 10G port can run at 1G. The reverse is not true — an SFP-only cage will not take SFP+.

**All of these are hot-swappable** — modules can be inserted or removed with the switch powered on.

## Connector types

**Fiber connector fundamentals:** every fiber connector centres the glass in a **ferrule** — a precision ceramic sleeve that aligns two fiber ends to within microns. Most fiber links need **two strands** (one to transmit, one to receive), so connectors come as **simplex** (single) or **duplex** (paired) — the exception being MPO, which carries many fibers in one ferrule.

### Subscriber connector (SC)
A **square, push-pull** connector with a **2.5 mm ferrule** — push in to seat, pull the housing to release. Nicknamed "stick and click" or "square connector."

Larger than LC, so it is being displaced in high-density installations, but still common in telco equipment, older enterprise installs, and ISP demarcation points. Available simplex or as a duplex clip holding two together.

### Local connector (LC)
A **small form factor** connector with a **1.25 mm ferrule** — half SC's diameter — using a **retaining clip** like an RJ45's, squeezed to release. Usually seen as a **duplex** pair.

Its size is the point: twice the port density of SC in the same panel space, which is why **LC is the standard on SFP/SFP+ transceivers** and the default for modern installations. If you plug fiber into a switch today, it is almost certainly LC.

### Straight tip (ST)
A **round, bayonet twist-lock** connector with a **2.5 mm ferrule** — push in and twist a quarter turn to lock, the same mechanism as BNC. Almost always **simplex**, so a duplex link needs two.

Largely **legacy**: common in 1990s multimode installations and still found in older campus and industrial buildings, but displaced by SC and then LC. Its bayonet lock is genuinely secure against vibration, which is why some industrial environments kept it.

**Identification:** ST is the round one that **twists**; SC is the square one that **pushes**. Both share a 2.5 mm ferrule.

### Multi-fiber push on (MPO)
A **rectangular, push-on** connector carrying **many fibers in a single ferrule** — commonly 12, 24, or 48 strands. Every other connector here handles one fiber per ferrule; MPO is the exception.

**Why it exists:** 40G and 100G optics use **parallel lanes**, not one fast stream. 40GBASE-SR4 sends 4 × 10 Gbps over four fiber pairs simultaneously — eight strands for one link. Terminating that as eight LC connectors would be absurd, so one MPO carries the lot.

**Where you meet it:** **QSFP+ and QSFP28 transceivers**, and high-density data-centre trunk cabling — a single MPO trunk replaces a bundle of duplex runs between racks.

**Practical notes:** MPO cables have a **polarity** (a key on the housing) that must match end to end, and **breakout/fanout** assemblies split one MPO into multiple LC duplex pairs — the cabling equivalent of a QSFP breakout cable.

**MTP** is a branded, higher-precision MPO — mechanically compatible, so the terms get used interchangeably.

### RJ11
The small **6-position** modular connector used for **telephone** and **DSL** — typically wired with 2 or 4 conductors (6P2C / 6P4C). Physically narrower than RJ45 and **not interchangeable** with it.

**Where it appears in networking:** the line side of a DSL modem, analog phone handsets, and fax lines. It carries no Ethernet.

**The trap worth knowing:** an RJ11 plug will physically fit *into* an RJ45 jack because it is narrower — it just seats crookedly and contacts the wrong pins. It never works, and forcing it can damage the jack's contacts. RJ45 into RJ11 is impossible; it is too wide.

### RJ45
The **8-position, 8-contact (8P8C)** modular connector used for **Ethernet over twisted pair** — the standard connector on every Cat5e/6/6a patch lead.

**Wiring standards:** **T568A** and **T568B** define which pair goes on which pin. Both work; **the two ends of a cable must match**. Same standard both ends = straight-through; different standards each end = crossover. T568B is the more common choice in North America.

**Why the pin order matters:** the standards keep each transmit/receive pair on a **properly twisted pair** of wires. Wiring pins in an order that splits a pair across two physical twists produces a **split pair** — it passes a continuity test and fails under load with crosstalk, which is why a cable tester that only checks continuity can pass a genuinely bad cable.

**Auto-MDIX** now negotiates transmit/receive roles automatically, so crossover cables are rarely needed on modern equipment.

### F-type
A **threaded screw-on** connector for **75 Ω coaxial** cable — cable TV and cable internet. Distinctive in that the **centre conductor of the coax itself serves as the pin**; there is no separate contact to crimp.

**Where you meet it:** the wall plate to your cable modem, and the drop from the street. It is the connector on the **WAN side of a cable modem** — everything past the modem is RJ45.

**Practical note:** finger-tight plus a light turn with a wrench is right. Loose F-connectors are a common cause of intermittent cable-internet faults, since a poor connection lets RF ingress interfere with the signal.

### Bayonet Neill–Concelman (BNC)
A **bayonet twist-lock** connector for coaxial cable — push on and twist a quarter turn to lock, the same mechanism as the fiber ST connector. Named after its two designers, Neill and Concelman.

**Where it appears:** legacy **10BASE2** Ethernet (50 Ω), analog **CCTV** video (75 Ω), and test equipment such as oscilloscopes. Its locking action resists vibration and accidental disconnection, which is why lab and broadcast gear kept using it.

**Impedance matters:** 50 Ω and 75 Ω BNC connectors look identical and mate physically, but mixing them causes signal reflections. The application dictates which is correct.

**Both bayonet connectors on this exam:** **BNC** on coax, **ST** on fiber. Same locking principle, different medium — check which medium a question describes before answering.
