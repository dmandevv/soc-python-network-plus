# 3.3 — Explain disaster recovery (DR) concepts

## DR metrics

### Recovery point objective (RPO) and recovery time objective (RTO)
Two measures defined by their relationship to the incident:

```
      RPO                incident                 RTO
  <----------------------  |  ---------------------->
  last good backup                         service restored
```

**RPO points backward; RTO points forward.**

**RPO — recovery point objective** is **how much data can be lost, expressed as time.** "We can tolerate losing at most one hour of transactions." **It determines backup frequency**, because everything written since the last backup is what is lost.

**RTO — recovery time objective** is **how long the service can be unavailable.** "We must be operational within four hours." **It determines recovery infrastructure** — an RTO of days permits restoring from backup, while an RTO of seconds requires something already running.

**⚠️ The two are independent and are frequently conflated:**

| Combination | Means |
|---|---|
| **Low RPO, high RTO** | Almost no data lost, but a day to become operational |
| **High RPO, low RTO** | Operational within minutes, having lost a day's work |

**⚠️ Both are business decisions, not technical ones.** The organisation determines what loss it can tolerate, and technology is then selected to meet that figure. Reasoning in the opposite direction — "backups run nightly, therefore our RPO is 24 hours" — describes the current capability rather than the requirement, and is how organisations discover their tolerance only after an incident.

**Cost rises steeply as either approaches zero:**

| RPO | Requires |
|---|---|
| 24 hours | Nightly backup |
| 1 hour | Hourly snapshots |
| **Near zero** | **Synchronous replication** |

| RTO | Requires |
|---|---|
| Days | Procure hardware, then restore |
| Hours | A warm site |
| **Seconds** | **Active-active cluster** |

**Neither objective should simply be "as low as possible."** Each is set where the cost of achieving it meets the cost of the outage it prevents.

**Mnemonic: RPO is the last good data *point* in the *past*; RTO is the *time* to restore.**

### Mean time to repair (MTTR) and mean time between failures (MTBF)
Two **measured** figures, in contrast to RPO and RTO, which are **chosen targets**.

| | Measures | Better when |
|---|---|---|
| **MTBF** | Average operating time **between** failures — reliability | **Higher** |
| **MTTR** | Average time taken **to restore** after a failure — maintainability | **Lower** |

**MTBF is typically a vendor figure** derived from testing, often quoted in tens or hundreds of thousands of hours. It is a statistical expectation across a population, **not a prediction about an individual device** — a 100,000-hour MTBF does not mean a given unit will run eleven years.

**MTTR is an organisational figure**, since it includes detection, diagnosis, parts availability, and the actual repair — not merely the hands-on work.

**Their relationship to availability:** `availability = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR)`. **Reducing MTTR often improves availability more cheaply than increasing MTBF**, because spares, documentation, and practised procedures cost far less than more reliable hardware.

## DR sites

| | Equipment | Data | Time to activate | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cold** | Space, power, cooling — **nothing installed** | None | **Days to weeks** | Lowest |
| **Warm** | Hardware installed and configured | **Periodically synchronised, not current** | **Hours** | Middle |
| **Hot** | Fully operational duplicate | **Continuously replicated** | **Minutes or less** | Highest |

**The distinguishing question is data, not hardware.** A warm site has the equipment ready but its data lags, so restoration is required before it can serve users. A hot site is already current and needs only to take over.

**The choice follows directly from RTO**, and the cost difference between the three is substantial.

## High-availability approaches

| | **Active-active** | **Active-passive** |
|---|---|---|
| Both nodes serving | ✅ Yes | ❌ Standby idle |
| Resource use | **Full** | Half the capacity idle |
| Failover | **Already handling traffic** | Standby must take over |
| Capacity after a failure | ⚠️ **One node carries everything** | Unchanged — designed for one |
| Complexity | Higher — state must be shared | Lower |

**⚠️ Active-active's principal risk is capacity, not failover.** If both nodes normally run at 60% utilisation, losing one leaves a single node facing 120% of its capacity — the failover succeeds and the service still fails. **Active-active must be sized so either node can carry the entire load alone**, which means neither exceeds 50% in normal operation.

**Active-passive wastes capacity by design**, and that waste is what guarantees the survivor can cope.

## Testing

| | **Tabletop exercise** | **Validation test** |
|---|---|---|
| Method | **Discussion** — no systems touched | **Actual execution** of the recovery |
| Tests | Whether the plan is understood and complete | Whether the plan **works** |
| Cost and risk | Low — a meeting | Higher; may require downtime |
| Finds | Gaps, unclear ownership, missing contacts | Broken backups, wrong assumptions, timing failures |

**Both are necessary because they find different faults.** A tabletop exercise reveals that nobody knows who authorises a failover; only a validation test reveals that the backups have been silently failing for four months.

**⚠️ An untested recovery plan is an assumption.** The most common discovery during a first validation test is that a restore does not work at all — which is precisely the failure that must not be discovered during an actual incident.
